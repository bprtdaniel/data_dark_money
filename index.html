Which Elections Take Dark-Money?

A visual look at where undisclosed political spending appeared in competitive 2024 House races.

Daniel Boppert, Luca Vellage 06/05/2026

"Donald Trump will work to lower your taxes, vote Trump."

What is the difference between those sentences?

According to U.S. campaign finance rules, the first is a direct endorsement of a candidate, making it political campaigning.

The second, however, can be framed as issue advocacy or “social welfare” activity rather than direct candidate support.

Under U.S. tax law, a 501(c)(4) organization is a nonprofit organization operated for social welfare purposes. These groups can spend money in politics, but they are not required to disclose donors in the same way traditional campaign committees or SuperPACs are.

They are often referred to as "Dark Money" Organizations.

05

Dark-Money in Close Elections

As of October 31st 2024, roughly 5 days before the election, eight districts were categorized as "Toss-Up", meaning not even a lean towards a party was identifiable, making them the closest races of all. All of these eight districts saw dark money activity.

06

Ohio’s 9th Congressional District

In an incredibly close election, incumbent Marcy Kaptur held on by about 0.7 points or roughly 2000 votes. At the same time, OpenSecrets reports that 400.000 dollars of dark money were spent in OH-09. Half of that is classified as "For Democrats", while the other is labelled "Against Republicans"

Dark Money is exactly what it says.

It succeeds in circumventing many or all disclosure policies, designed to keep the political system free from corruption and foreign influence.

The system is designed to be complicated and opaque and 'Creative Accounting' and landmark judicial decisions like Citizens United further flooded politics with money by removing campaign finance limits.

While dark money may not be the tipping point in competitive races already afloat with cash, it uncovers the systems vulnerability.
